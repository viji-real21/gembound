The claim, up front

A pool of subscription coding agents, scheduled by arithmetic instead of hope, covers 100% of around-the-clock development demand at $319/month — where the same money spent as two plain subscriptions covers 63%. The gap is not model quality. It is scheduling.

Framed honestly: this is proven by simulation — a 70-test contract suite over a discrete-event backtest of demand traces — not a mathematical theorem about all futures.

Scheduler

Arithmetic at every layer

Five rules stand between the queue and the bill. Each one runs before a model is ever asked to think.

01

Meters before model calls

Every lane's burn is checked before work starts.

Hourly, daily and weekly meters. A refused task costs zero tokens.
02

Leak stops same-day

A runaway agent is parked automatically, the same day.

First catch: 13%/hr with no output. 2%/hour audits; 4% and 7% soft stops, 12% and 15% hard stops.
03

Overnight unattended missions

Headless builder plus an independent critic per phase.

Every morning: done / evidence / blocked. 45 logged episodes, measured quality 0.87–0.92 on the free lane.
04

Warm sessions, bounded context

Compaction points solved from the provider's own price ratio.

K* = b + √(2·(f/c)·(b+R)·d) — cutting a measured burn by 42%.

What it costs to be honest

The 100% is a simulation result

The 100% figure is a simulation result under modeled demand, re-run as a contract test on every change. Heavy real-world weeks can still starve a lane; the scheduler's promise is that starvation, when it happens, is visible and priced, not silent.
